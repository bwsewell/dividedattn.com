title: "Paw Prints"
date: 2026-07-08
---

My daughter started third grade and came home the other day with a school-issued Chromebook.  We were completely unprepared for it, which feels silly to admit in the grand scheme of things.  It's a Chromebook.  Millions of kids have them.  But in our house, this is the first device that is *hers*, and we had done absolutely nothing to get ready for that.

That's not an accident.  We've been deliberate about this stuff for years.  She can use the family iPad to watch movies on long road trips, and that's about the extent of it.  She doesn't use our phones.  She doesn't browse the internet or wander around YouTube.  We've read the same research everyone else has about social media and developing brains, and now the AI warning labels are stacking up right next to it, with people drawing a straight line from one to the other.  So we've held the line, and it's been a pretty easy line to hold, right up until the school district handed her a laptop and a login.

Then this happened.  She and a classmate found paw prints on the playground.  They wanted to know what animal made them, so they hatched a plan: print out pictures of different animal paw prints, bring them to school the next day, and compare.  And she asked if she could figure out how to do that on her Chromebook.

She knows what Gemini is.  She knows what Google is.  I'm not sure she actually knows how to use either one yet.  But standing there listening to her describe the plan, it genuinely felt like the right window to introduce these tools.  Two kids found something mysterious in the physical world, formed a question, and wanted to gather evidence to answer it.  The technology wasn't replacing the curiosity.  It was in service of it.  My generation would have done the same thing with a library card and an encyclopedia, and I don't think the encyclopedia version is automatically more virtuous just because it was slower.

But I'd be lying if I said I wasn't nervous.  She has ADHD, and for her, "researching something on the internet" can be a rabbit hole with no bottom.  One search becomes ten, and the original question dissolves somewhere along the way.  That's not a kid problem, either.  It happens to me.

And that's really the thing.  I am not as optimistic as some people are about what AI is going to do to the generation that's in elementary school right now.  I'm truly worried about the knock-on effects of outsourcing critical thinking to a large language model before you've built any critical thinking to outsource.  As an adult, I can already feel the pull toward it in myself — the little moment where I could reason through something and instead I just ask.  Noticing that tendency in me has made me hypersensitive to any technology I bring into the house for her.

So I've been trying to build a small habit around that pull.  Every time I offload a task to AI, I stop and ask myself, honestly: what did I just miss out on?  What opportunity did I give up by not doing that myself?  Sometimes the answer is nothing — some work is just toil, and my life is no poorer for skipping it.  But sometimes the answer stings.  A problem I would have understood more deeply if I'd wrestled with it.  A skill that got a little duller because I didn't use it.  AI is everywhere, and it's only going to be more everywhere, and literacy around it is going to matter for our kids the way reading does.  But I'm starting to think the core of that literacy isn't knowing how to prompt.  It's knowing how to ask that question — and answering it honestly.

A couple of years ago, after having an Alexa in the house for eight years, we got rid of it.  We walked past the playroom one day and discovered our daughter having a conversation with it.  It was playing a guessing game with her.  We have no idea how she unlocked that feature or what she said to get there.  Nothing bad happened.  But something about it startled us — this strange, passive form of entertainment, a machine keeping our kid company — and that was enough.  The Alexas went in a drawer and never came back out.

So now there's a Chromebook in the house, and I'm trying to figure out where the paw prints end and the guessing game begins.  I don't have a tidy answer.  The best I've got is that the difference seems to be who's holding the question.  When she walks up to the technology with something she wants to know, it's a tool.  When the technology starts generating the entertainment and she's just along for the ride, it's something else.

We printed the paw prints.
